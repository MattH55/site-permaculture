Regenerating an Alberta Acreage: Water First, Then Food Forest

A design-build story of contour earthworks, zone planning, and cold-climate plantings — the Expanding Edge approach landowners hire us for across Western Canada.

  • FocusWater harvesting · zones · food forest
  • DeliveryDesign + earthworks + planting
  • ClimateShort season · wind · variable precip

The challenge

Like many Alberta holdings, the property had productive potential but conventional layout: runoff leaving the site too quickly, wind stress on open ground, and planting areas that fought the land instead of using slope and aspect. The owners wanted food production, wildlife habitat, and a place that would improve — not degrade — over decades. They did not want a plan that never left the binder.

Design approach

Expanding Edge started with observation and data: elevation and slope, aspect, wet spots, access, and energy flows (sun, wind, cold air drainage). The design stacked classic permaculture priorities for cold-climate Western Canada:

  1. Water first — slow, spread, and sink; hold moisture in soil and strategic storage
  2. Zones & access — frequent-use areas near the home; extensive systems farther out
  3. Shelter & edges — windbreaks and productive edges before high-value plantings
  4. Phased plantings — hardy structure first; guild density as soil and microclimates improve

The same pipeline we use for Edmonton Capital Region and Calgary foothills projects: map → design → implement → steward.

Implementation: earthworks

Contour swales and related earthworks were prioritized early. On Alberta clay-loam and loam sites, holding spring melt and summer storms in the soil profile is often the highest-return investment: it supports plantings, reduces erosion, and builds resilience in dry years. Work was staked to design, installed with care for overflow paths, and paired with access that would not undo the hydrology.

Implementation: food forest guilds

Planting followed water work. Guilds used cold-hardy trees and shrubs suited to parkland and prairie-edge conditions, with understory species that build soil, support pollinators, and stack yields. Establishment included mulch, protection where needed, and a plan for years two and three — not a single “plant and pray” day.

Outcomes

Hydrology

Runoff slowed and infiltrated on contour; wet and dry areas became design assets rather than problems to ignore.

Productivity path

Zones set up for annuals near the house and perennial systems expanding outward as labour and capital allow.

Living proof

Earthworks and plantings are visible and functional — the standard we hold as we scale implementation across Canada.

Lessons for Alberta & Western Canada landowners

  • Sequence matters. Earthworks before expensive plantings usually wins in windy, water-variable climates.
  • Phase honestly. A 10-year regenerative plan beats a one-season fantasy budget.
  • Local climate is non-negotiable. Chinooks, frost pockets, and clay behave differently than coastal permaculture media suggests.
  • Design-build reduces loss. When the same team (or tightly coordinated partners) digs what was drawn, fewer errors compound on the ground.
